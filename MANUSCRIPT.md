## Abstract
This study explores how first-year Master of Social Work (MSW) students experience mentorship at a small private university, addressing the gap between the recognized value of mentorship and its lack of formal implementation. Grounded in the theoretical framework of critical pedagogy, the research views mentorship as a collaborative tool for professional empowerment and social justice. A qualitative research design was employed, utilizing semi-structured interviews with a purposive sample of five MSW students. 

The findings reveal that mentorship currently functions as an informal, faculty-dependent, and peer-driven experience. Students identified professors as primary navigators of professional identity, placing a high importance on responsive communication and career role modeling. However, non-traditional and remote students face significant barriers, often acting as their own blockers due to competing life responsibilities and a lack of integrated support. 

The study highlights critical implications for social work education, advocating for a shift from reactive to proactive support models. Key recommendations include embedding mentorship in the required curriculum, establishing formal near-peer mentoring programs to alleviate faculty burden, and adopting national accreditation standards for mentorship accessibility. By shifting the burden of support from individual students to the institution, MSW programs can foster a more equitable and sustainable environment for emerging social work professionals.

## Introduction & Background of the Problem
Master of Social Work (MSW) academic programs at colleges and universities in the United States involve a diverse blend of learning experiences. Across academic settings and field placements, a wide variety of lessons unfold as theories are applied. Another channel for learning is mentorship, a relationship that promotes collaboration and the pursuit of growth goals, helping bridge the gap between theory and practice in social work.

Mentorship is an aspect of social work education that risks being overlooked. Although mentorship in the social work field is widely recognized as particularly important for professional development and training, there is no explicit requirement or obligation to provide it. Consequently, students miss out on its potential when opportunities are not offered through school faculty or field supervisors (Pomeroy & Steiker, 2011). A lack of formal, structured mentorship systems in MSW programs can leave emerging social work professionals without adequate career development support.

### Conceptual Definitions
To explore the issue of MSW mentorship, a few definitions of key concepts are necessary:
* **Mentoring:** A relationship in which a more experienced social worker (the mentor) nurtures the growth of a less experienced person (the mentee) by supporting their professional and psychosocial development (Skrzypek et al., 2019).
* **Evidence-Based Practices:** A five-step process in social work practice that includes formulating questions, searching for evidence, engaging in critical thinking, factoring evidence into practice, and measuring outcomes (Weller et al., 2022).
* **Distributed Learning:** A nontraditional format featuring online or hybrid educational programs that increase access for students who are from historically underserved backgrounds (Jensen, 2017).
* **Near-Peer Mentoring:** A mentorship model in which more advanced students (e.g., doctoral or advanced MSW students) mentor students earlier in their education (MSW or bachelor’s students) (Schuman et al., 2021).

### Scope of the Problem
Empirical data outlines the broad scope of this institutional gap. According to the 2023–2024 Council on Social Work Education (CSWE) annual survey of social work programs, 225 responding MSW programs reported the following demographic metrics, reflecting the extensive student baseline affected by these concerns (CSWE, 2025):
* **55,935** MSW students enrolled in the academic year.
* **2,471** students who are first-generation at the master's level.
* **60.8%** full-time students, and **39.2%** part-time students.
* **81.4%** female students, **15.0%** male, **3.1%** unknown, and **0.5%** other.

Although some MSW departments maintain formal mentoring programs, there is little evidence that they are a standard feature. As recently as 2019, an implemented formal mentoring program noted it had no operational precedent for its model design (Skrzypek et al., 2019). This reflects two underlying problems: first, there is a lack of understanding related to structured formats that can be used to provide mentorship to students, leaving a distinct knowledge gap in program design, implementation, and evaluation; second, the current momentum of this problem leans heavily toward maintaining the status quo.

As the issue stands today, the trend will likely be for many MSW programs to continue relying on the traditional, informal approach to student mentorship. This approach emphasizes informal mentorship by faculty and field supervisors. However, resource and time constraints frequently limit these individuals' capacity to provide consistent guidance. This remains an unreliable approach that lacks equity due to inconsistent access. 

It has been observed that up to half of the students in this setting lack a mentor entirely (Skrzypek et al., 2019; Stafford & Robbins, 1991). Furthermore, even when it is provided, mentorship guidance can lean more administrative than developmental given the status quo. Without intentional effort to address these issues, a gap in professional development will persist, disproportionately affecting at-risk, online, male, first-generation, and minority students (Jensen, 2017; Rogers, 2013; Schuman et al., 2021).

## Purpose of the Study & Research Question
The purpose of this study is to explore how first-year MSW students think about and experience mentorship at a small private university. Examining their feedback can inform the design and implementation of formal mentorship models to support their professional development, meet academic expectations, and address the needs of students from diverse backgrounds. By engaging in further study, this research will guide recommendations for better supporting students through the integration of theory and practice in mentorship.

The primary research question guiding this investigation is:  
> *“To what extent does the presence of a mentorship relationship, whether formal or informal, influence the development of professional identity and academic persistence of first-year MSW students at a small private university?”*

### Significance of the Study
Considering the value of mentorship, this study aims to explore ways to make mentorship more consistently accessible to MSW students. Seeking to understand the perceptions and experiences of first-year MSW students at a small private university will help identify specific barriers and opportunities for student success. This builds on existing research across three levels of significance:

* **Theoretical Significance:** This study adds unique value to the literature by emphasizing qualitative individual interviews to explore students' deep experiences, contrasting with prior research that typically gathers information via broad surveys and focus groups. It develops an understanding of mentorship within the underrepresented context of a small private university, prioritizing equity for historically underserved students.
* **Practical Significance:** The findings provide practical aid to social work practitioners and program directors. It develops evidence-based recommendations to transition mentorship from merely administrative to developmental check-ins, facilitating retention and informing alternative near-peer or e-mentoring models.
* **Policy Significance:** This study advocates for the inclusion of mentorship in organizational policies, strategy, and procedures. It highlights how formal mentorship can factor into institutional resource allocation, accreditation benchmarks, and programmatic strategies to elevate student belonging.

## Theoretical Framework: Critical Pedagogy
Mentorship in MSW programs can be best understood through a theory that explains the approaches used. A theoretical framework helps with considering how and why a mentorship model can boost professional development, shaping the design, interpretation, and translation of theory into practice.

### Overview of Relevant Theory
Critical pedagogy challenges traditional approaches to education, which can be seen as channels for social inequality and power imbalances. It addresses these structural biases by fostering a collaborative, inclusive learning environment (Billings, 2024). 

*Pedagogy of the Oppressed* (1970), the foundational text on critical pedagogy by Paulo Freire, centered the framework on several key ideas drawn from literacy education in Brazil. Core concepts include:
1. **Critical Consciousness (*Conscientização*):** Recognizing one's reality as an oppressive structure to be critically addressed and transformed.
2. **Problem-Posing Education vs. The Banking Concept:** Rejecting the "banking" model where passive students simply receive and store information deposits from an all-knowing authority, replacing it with empowering, reciprocal dialogue.
3. **Praxis:** The continuous loop of reflection and action directed toward transforming social structures.
4. **Mutual Learning:** Education as a horizontal process of co-learning, mutual understanding, and deep trust.

Over time, other theorists expanded this baseline. Henry Giroux (1985) reinforced the value of knowledge derived from personal experience, elevating its unique teaching potential when brought into critical question. bell hooks (1994) integrated intersectionality theory into critical pedagogy, emphasizing how race, gender, and socioeconomic status intersect within the educational process. Applying these elements creates an approach to learning that serves as a vehicle for social justice and collective emancipation (Billings, 2024; Kridel, 2010).

### Prior Applications in Mentorship Literature
The intersection of critical pedagogy and mentorship is supported by prior qualitative research:
* **Hall (2015):** Utilized critical pedagogy as a conceptual framework for a mentoring program for African American adolescent males. The study found that critical pedagogy served as a culturally responsive intervention that improved students' personal agency, challenged power imbalances, and maximized strengths-based interventions.
* **Lorenzetti et al. (2019):** Evaluated a social work mentorship program focused on transformative learning for MSW students through relationships with community mentors. The results demonstrated that framing mentorship through critical pedagogy fostered motivation, critical dialogue, community development, and a shared orientation toward social justice.

### Relevance to the Current Study
In this study, critical pedagogy serves as a conceptual framework for understanding mentorship in an empowering, collaborative, and inclusive way. It frames the mentor-mentee relationship as a mutual process of education and dialogue capable of dismantling traditional hierarchy. 

Rather than treating professional identity and academic persistence as isolated, individual metrics, this lens views them as outcomes embedded within larger institutional systems. Utilizing qualitative semi-structured interviews directly embodies critical pedagogy by validating knowledge derived from personal student experiences, highlighting equity by identifying hidden structural barriers.

## Literature Review
This literature review explores the role of mentorship in established MSW programs, drawing patterns across program design, implementation, strengths, and systemic challenges.

### Benefits of MSW Mentorship Programs
The valuable role of mentorship in social work education is well-documented, despite its omission from the explicit requirements of the CSWE Educational Policy and Accreditation Standards (Schuman et al., 2021). Formal mentorship designs yield improved grades, higher retention and graduation rates, greater self-efficacy, and a stronger professional identity (Jensen, 2017; Schuman et al., 2021). It has also been shown to improve research competence by increasing students’ engagement with the evidence-based practice process (Weller et al., 2022).

Beyond academic performance, mentorship provides essential psychosocial support and socialization. Mentors create a safe space for reflecting on clinical and field challenges, building confidence and fostering a sense of community belonging (Skrzypek et al., 2019). This function is vital for non-traditional, older, male, and first-generation students who manage competing professional and family obligations and encounter higher systematic barriers to success (Jensen, 2017; Rogers, 2013; Schuman et al., 2021). 

Crucially, successful mentoring relationships are highly reciprocal (Lorenzetti et al., 2019). Faculty and alumni mentors report significant mutual benefits, including personal satisfaction, a renewed professional social work identity, ongoing skill development, and a meaningful reconnection to their academic institutions (Skrzypek et al., 2019).

### Operational Mentorship Models
The literature identifies a range of operational models, each carrying unique advantages and trade-offs:
* **Formal Student-Alumni & Peer Models:** Endorsed as an equitable means to ensure all students access support, bypassing the bias and inconsistent access associated with organic, informal relationships (Jensen, 2017; Mitschke et al., 2021; Skrzypek et al., 2019). However, these models demand substantial administrative time, matching effort, and institutional resources to implement.
* **E-Mentoring:** Delivered electronically via text, phone, and virtual meetings. This has proven highly effective for geographically dispersed, remote, or at-risk students because technology successfully bridges structural scheduling conflicts (Jensen, 2017; Mitschke et al., 2021; Schuman et al., 2021).
* **Near-Peer and Nested Mentoring:** Utilizes advanced MSW or doctoral students to mentor early-stage students. This model leverages shared student experiences and collaborative learning while actively developing leadership competencies in the mentors (Inoue et al., 2017; Schuman et al., 2021).

### Challenges and Gaps in Existing Literature
A recurring barrier in implementing successful MSW mentorship programs is a lack of clarity, leading to role confusion, ambiguous communication guidelines, and insufficient participant commitment (Jensen, 2017; Skrzypek et al., 2019). Under-resourced environments frequently suffer from communication uncertainty, missed programmatic opportunities, and premature discontinuation of the mentoring pair (Lee et al., 2026; Skrzypek et al., 2019).

Furthermore, the existing literature suffers from distinct methodological limitations. Much of the current data relies on small, homogeneous samples and cross-sectional survey designs, restricting generalizability and the capacity to move beyond mere correlation to establish causal mechanisms (Jensen, 2017; Schuman et al., 2021; Weller et al., 2022). There is a pronounced gap in understanding long-term persistence and qualitative data regarding the deep mentoring relationship itself. 

The current study contributes directly to filling this gap by shifting away from focus groups and broad surveys toward intensive, semi-structured individual interviews within a small private university setting—a context heavily underrepresented in social work research.

## Methodology

### Research Design
This study utilized a qualitative research design to obtain rich, detailed descriptions of the lived experiences of first-year MSW students. Qualitative methods are uniquely suited to exploring complex social interactions, personal perspectives, and surrounding institutional environments. 

The specific method employed was semi-structured interviewing, which provides a flexible yet consistent framework for open-ended inquiry and clarification follow-ups. This design allows patterns and themes to emerge directly from the participants' organic accounts rather than imposing a predetermined hypothesis, a methodology well-supported by prior literature in social work education (Miehls et al., 2013; Mitschke et al., 2021).

### Participants & Purposive Sampling
A purposive sample of graduate students was recruited from a small, private Midwestern university in the United States. The target population was strictly limited to first-year MSW students pursuing professional graduate training, as the first academic year represents a critical friction point for professional identity formation and academic persistence. 

* **Inclusion Criteria:** Current enrollment in the university's MSW program, ensuring active experience engaging with department faculty and peer groups.
* **Exclusion Criteria:** Students outside the MSW department, students beyond their first year of study, and students enrolled in the accelerated 3+2 blended undergraduate/graduate track. This strict bounding reduced confounding variables related to multi-year adjustment and provided a homogenous cohort for interpretive comparison.

Recruitment was coordinated through the MSW Program Director, who identified 16 eligible students and sent an institutional recruitment email. Participation was entirely voluntary. Interested students contacted the director, who provided a formal warm introduction to the student researcher via email. A sample size of five active first-year students was secured for the final interview phase.

### Data Collection Procedures
Data collection procedures were formally approved by the Grand View University Institutional Review Board (IRB #405-26) under expedited review status (Categories 6 and 7), as the protocol involved minimal risk and relied on standard voice recordings and semi-structured dialogue (see Appendix A). Prior to initiating dialogue, the researcher executed a strict informed consent process (see Appendix B), detailing the project's academic purpose, voluntary nature, and privacy rights. Confidentiality was safeguarded by separating all signed consent forms from transcription records and storing data files on a secure, password-protected computer. 

The data collection cycle followed a systematic five-step sequence:
1. **Recruitment:** Program director identified the 16 eligible students and dispatched the recruitment script.
2. **Introduction:** Interested subjects were introduced to the researcher via email to arrange secure scheduling.
3. **Preparation:** The researcher deployed a standardized five-prompt interview guide to maintain systematic structural alignment (see Appendix C).
4. **Interview Sessions:** Five individual interviews, lasting up to 30 minutes each, were conducted in private campus conference rooms or via secure video conferencing.
5. **Documentation:** Raw audio files were transcribed word-for-word into text assets utilizing Otter AI software (see Appendix D).

### Data Analysis
The qualitative analysis followed a systematic, four-step inductive thematic coding approach to translate raw transcriptions into interpretive themes without forcing data into predetermined theoretical blocks (see Appendix E):
1. **Immersive Reading:** The researcher read straight through all transcription assets continuously to establish a holistic baseline understanding of the content.
2. **Pattern Identification:** A second close reading was performed to generate margin codes, extracting repeating key words, phrases, and semantic properties.
3. **Refining Patterns:** The preliminary margin codes were synthesized and organized into major thematic structures observed across participants.
4. **Detailed Breakdown:** The researcher isolated granular subthemes and documented unique participant anomalies that stood out from the baseline content cluster.

This iterative analysis generated three major overarching themes, with each theme supported by three distinct subthemes.

---

## Findings

### Theme 1: Institutional Guidance and Faculty Accessibility
The first theme documents the reliance on university staff and professors as primary navigators for students. Faculty contact consistently extended past administrative check-ins into deep psychosocial support and work-life balance navigation.

* **Faculty as Primary Contacts:** Participants uniformly identified professors as their immediate go-to resource for academic and career advancement queries. Participant A emphasized that they closely “leaned on” a specific professor for personal stability, while Participant C described a primary professor as a “fabulous” resource who functioned as their explicit “number one person”.
* **Responsive Communication Pacing:** The speed of faculty feedback loops emerged as a critical driver of student self-efficacy. Participant A labeled the department's communication culture as “spot on,” highlighting that responses consistently arrived in “less than 24 hours”. Participant C mirrored this data, reporting that their primary faculty contact typically responded “within the same day,” significantly mitigating student anxiety.
* **Administrative vs. Developmental Support:** A clear divergence emerged regarding what students desired from faculty interactions. Participant B focused heavily on organizational logistics, utilizing corporate-style tracking terms such as navigating “admissions,” completing “commencement” benchmarks, and getting paperwork “finalized”. Conversely, Participant A sought deep developmental role-modeling, stating they turned to a specific professor because their historical career path was explicitly *"something that I want to do"*.

### Theme 2: The Non-Traditional Student Experience
The second theme captures the complex operational realities of students balancing multi-role identities against thin institutional margins of error.

* **Balancing Multiple Identities:** Most subjects detailed intense stressors related to work-life compression. Participant D outlined the lived reality of navigating an accelerated graduate curriculum as a “mom of two” entering social work as a “second career,” frequently managing acute disruptions like a sudden “family emergency”. For Participant B, adjusting to these simultaneous professional and domestic expectations felt like “jumping into a great big pool with both feet”.
* **Isolation in Online Learning Environments:** The virtual classroom framework presented clear technical and personal barriers to relationship building. Participant A, attending fully remotely, noted the isolating experience of “talking to the whole room” through a screen setup, which complicated forming deep peer rapport. Participant B highlighted a steep technology “learning curve” associated with managing online educational systems after an extended decade-long absence from higher education.
* **Low Margin of Error:** The financial and personal stakes of the MSW track create highly vulnerable academic paths. Participant C explicitly weighed the personal investment required against programmatic uncertainty, asking, “Is it worth it?”. Furthermore, resource constraints often cause students to become their own “blockers”. Participant D explained that balancing their “full life” left zero capacity to seek out university support services, rendering available resources inaccessible because they were detached from the immediate academic workflow.

### Theme 3: Peer Support versus Formal Mentorship
The third theme details how students organically build informal horizontal networks to compensate for a lack of formal, structured institutional pathways.

* **Peer Connection and Horizontal Solidarity:** Classmates frequently stepped in to fill structural information gaps. Participant D emphasized the protective value of “texting classmates” to track curriculum deadlines and handle emotional stress through raw peer “solidarity”. Participant E highlighted that embedded “large group projects” served as vital spaces for establishing mutual “trust” and student “empathy”.
* **Desire for Near-Peer Mentoring:** The data revealed an untapped demand for guidance from peers situated just a few steps ahead in the curriculum. Participant B suggested implementing a structured “peer mentorship program” modeled after corporate onboarding frameworks, noting that near-peers offer unique benefits because they possess a “refreshed mind” and high comfort with educational technology.
* **Self-Reliance and Forced Autonomy:** In the absence of a formal match, students were frequently forced to operate in isolation. Participant E detailed an individual reliance where they simply “powered through” severe organizational volatility. Participant D recounted feeling entirely on their own when making critical field decisions regarding client “safety” and tracking “complex behaviors”. The resulting emotional pressure caused Participant A to "freak out for a couple of hours" during a stressful operational bottleneck, summarizing the common risk of being left to *"figure out on my own"*.

## Discussion & Implications

### Interpretation of Findings
The qualitative data demonstrates that the status quo for mentorship at this small private university is an informal, faculty-dependent, and peer-driven experience rather than a structured program. While a small-university setting offers high baseline faculty accessibility, a distinct mentorship gap persists for remote and non-traditional learners.

First, in the absence of a formal matching framework, professors act as structural "first responders" rather than simple classroom instructors. As Participant C shared, an accessible professor becomes a student's “number one person” for professional transition. Rapid communication response cycles—such as the "less than 24 hours" culture praised by Participant A—directly elevate student self-assurance and lower anxiety. However, these relationships yield the highest developmental value when they cross past daily administrative transactions into long-term career role-modeling and vision building.

Second, non-traditional students navigate a compressed timeline. Remote delivery models present unexpected barriers, such as the intimidating "fishbowl effect" noted by Participant A where a student must "talk to the whole room" through an open speaker system, chilling organic connection. Students act as their own blockers because a "full life" leaves no time to locate external resources. Without proactive psychosocial scaffolding, this extreme strain prompts existential retention risks, causing students to ask, "Is it worth it?".

Lastly, students organically fill institutional voids via horizontal solidarity. Classmate texting groups function as vital protective shields against systemic burnout. There is a clear demand for near-peer frameworks to leverage multi-level experience across cohorts. Shifting the burden of support from vulnerable individuals who are forced to "power through" alone to an integrated institutional system is an immediate requirement for equity.

### Connection to Theory and Literature
Integrating critical pedagogy reframes these findings, clarifying that student reliance on faculty and peers represents collaborative problem-posing education rather than a traditional, passive banking model. Linking the Freirean concept of praxis to professional identity reveals that students seek a career vision that merges reflection with action.

Furthermore, critical pedagogy mandates that the acute barriers faced by remote and non-traditional students must be categorized as **systemic institutional failures rather than individual character deficiencies**. In alignment with the literature, the absence of a formal framework disproportionately penalizes students who already manage an uphill climb in higher education (Pomeroy & Steiker, 2011; Skrzypek et al., 2019).

In contrast to large-scale studies that cite organizational resource scarcity as the primary barrier to mentorship, this small-university sample highlights that the primary barrier is the student's compressed capacity to seek out detached support. Therefore, if sources of mentorship remain optional or unintegrated into the immediate academic experience, they remain functionally inaccessible to the students who need them most.

### Multi-Level Implications for Practice, Policy, and Research

#### 1. Implications for Social Work Practice
Practitioners, faculty advisors, and field supervisors must intentionally balance administrative task tracking with developmental mentoring. Leaders should initiate proactive, structured check-ins regarding long-term career visions rather than waiting for isolated trainees to reach out for assistance. Maintaining rapid, empathetic, and responsive communication pacing directly validates emerging social workers and reinforces their professional self-efficacy.

#### 2. Implications for Institutional Policy
Structural changes are required across all three levels of social work systems:
* **Micro-Level Policy:** Embed formal mentorship directly into the required first-year MSW student experience. Making mentorship a credit-bearing, required program feature ensures inclusion across all student backgrounds, bypassing the barrier of limited time.
* **Mezzo-Level Policy:** Academic social work departments should implement formal near-peer mentoring programs. This shifts the burden away from faculty and creates a nested support system where students at different phases of the MSW curriculum can share knowledge and solidarity.
* **Macro-Level Policy:** National accreditation standards, such as those of the CSWE, should adopt metrics for mentorship accessibility to ensure equitable support across all MSW programs.

#### 3. Implications for Future Research
Future social work inquiry must expand via longitudinal designs to track individuals' long-term persistence beyond first-year education and graduation, measuring the correlation between early interventions and long-term career retention. Researchers should study the experiences of mentors directly—including faculty and peer mentors—to understand time constraints and perspectives on this mutual learning process. Additionally, comparative studies should be conducted to evaluate the best methods for service delivery, comparing built-in required curriculum methods against optional opt-in models.

## Limitations & Conclusion

### Limitations of the Study
An interpretation of the findings must consider several limitations of this study, which provide vital context for this research and set the stage for future work:
1. **Sample Size:** Having a purposive sample of only five first-year MSW participants limits the range of lived experiences captured.
2. **Generalizability:** The results have limited generalizability, as they are centered on a single small private university in the Midwestern United States and may not apply to larger public institutions or to academic programs in other regions.
3. **Participant Diversity:** A lack of participant diversity limits the findings. The purposive sample did not reflect a wide range of demographics and life circumstances, and students from marginalized backgrounds may be underrepresented.
4. **Data Collection Constraints:** The use of semi-structured interviews introduced additional limitations, such as question design that can limit the range of responses, researcher bias in data interpretation, and shortcomings of virtual data collection via video conferencing.
5. **Cross-Sectional Framework:** A cross-sectional study was the final limitation, as it provided only a snapshot of student perceptions and experiences rather than tracking shifting variables longitudinally.

### Conclusion
This study explored the inner workings of mentorship within an MSW academic program, including the gap between its recognized value and the lack of a formal system to provide it. Currently, mentorship functions as an informal, faculty-dependent, peer-driven experience rather than a structured or required program. Emerging social workers look to faculty as critical anchors for professional identity, heavily prioritizing rapid, responsive communication and career role modeling over transactional administrative tracking alone. 

Non-traditional and remote students navigate a compressed learning space, facing significant hidden structural barriers like limited access to resources, conflicting family or career duties, and the online audio fishbowl effect. Looking ahead, psychosocial support and horizontal solidarity can be successfully drawn from near-peer relationships and informal classmate networks. To bridge the gap between classroom theory and future professional field competence, MSW programs must deploy critical pedagogy praxis, systematically shifting the burden of mentorship support away from vulnerable individual students and onto the institution through formal, proactive, and embedded mentorship models.
